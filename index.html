UBERvsGRAB

Same business model. Two very different stages.

One company turned the rideshare platform into a cash machine. The other is racing to get there, and it dominates a market most Americans have never used. This is a comparison of where each one stands, and where each may be headed over the next 6 to 24 months.

01 / The question

Why Grab?

As a Chinese-Filipino, this research topic felt personal and important to me. Before visiting the Philippines, I had never used Grab and barely knew the company existed. As someone who regularly uses Uber in the United States, I assumed the rideshare market would look similar everywhere else. Instead, I found that Grab was integrated into everyday life in a way that felt very different from Uber.

During my trip, I used Grab not only for transportation, but also for food delivery and digital payments. Conversations with friends and family from Vietnam and Thailand revealed the same pattern. In many parts of Southeast Asia, Grab is not simply a rideshare company. It has become a platform that people rely on for a variety of daily services.

That experience made me curious about the business behind the app. How does Grab compare to Uber, the company that helped popularize ridesharing and once competed directly in Southeast Asia?

In 2018, Uber sold its Southeast Asian operations to Grab and left the region in exchange for an ownership stake in the company. Since then, the two firms have followed very different paths.
02 / The market

The Southeast Asia opportunity

Grab operates as a “superapp,” combining ridesharing, food delivery, and digital financial services in one platform across eight countries. As a leading platform in the region, its advantage is a large, young population that is increasingly relying on digital services, where a strong platform can strengthen its position as more users join.

Stock performance

Five years, two opposite trajectories

Indexed to a starting value of 100, Uber climbed to about 141, a 41% gain, while Grab fell to about 28, a 72% loss. Grab also carried more risk, with a higher weekly standard deviation and a single −42% week in early 2022. That extra volatility produced lower, not higher, returns.

Fundamentals

Same growth, very different cash

In 2024 both companies grew revenue by roughly 18%. But their ability to turn that revenue into cash was worlds apart: Uber converted 15.7% of revenue into free cash flow versus just 4.9% for Grab, or $6.9 billion against $136 million. The numbers suggest Uber has already reached a more mature stage of growth, while Grab is still investing heavily to expand.

Valuation

What the market is paying for

Because Grab is not yet profitable, the P/E ratio doesn’t apply. On price-to-sales and price-to-free-cash-flow, Grab actually trades higher, at 4.8× sales and 99× free cash flow, against Uber’s 3.3× and 21×. Investors appear to expect much stronger future growth from Grab, while Uber’s valuation is supported more by the profits and cash flow it already produces.

03 / The verdict

The investment takeaway

Uber
Stability

Profitable, generating substantial free cash flow, still growing near 18%, and trading at reasonable multiples. Its main long-term risk is the rise of autonomous vehicles, which could eventually reshape rideshare economics.

Outlook: well positioned over the next two years.
Grab
Upside, with risk

A bet on Southeast Asia’s digital growth. Its elevated valuation already assumes strong future profits. If Grab keeps converting growth into cash flow, returns could be strong; if growth slows, the valuation becomes hard to justify.

Outlook: promising, but dependent on stronger profits and cash flow.

Two companies, one business model, two stages, and a reminder that growth only creates value once it turns into cash.

Methodology & limitations

Weekly returns were computed from five years of adjusted closing prices. Fundamentals use full-year 2024 figures from each company’s official filings. A few caveats: Uber’s 2024 net income was inflated by a one-time $6.4B tax benefit, so operating income better reflects the business; the two firms define free cash flow slightly differently; Grab’s shorter trading history affects its early returns; and past performance does not guarantee future results.
